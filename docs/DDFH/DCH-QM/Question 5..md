### Short answer

**No.**  
While the orthodox (Copenhagen) formulation of quantum mechanics takes the Born probabilities as *fundamental*—the wavefunction does not contain a deterministic sub‑state—this is *not* the only place in physics where probabilities are treated as primary.  

In almost every other theory probability is a *tool* that represents incomplete knowledge, not an ontological fact.  Quantum mechanics is the only widely‑accepted theory that, in its standard interpretation, **requires** probabilities as the most fundamental statement about reality, but that is a *choice of interpretation*, not a logical necessity.  If you adopt a different interpretation (Bohmian mechanics, many‑worlds, objective collapse, etc.) the underlying dynamics are deterministic and the Born rule becomes an emergent, statistical feature—exactly what DDFH proposes.

## 1.  Probabilities in physics: *tool* vs. *fundamental*

| Field | Use of probability | Status |
|-------|--------------------|--------|
| **Classical statistical mechanics** | Gibbs/Maxwell–Boltzmann distributions describe the fraction of micro‑states; they are *epistemic* (ignorance of the exact micro‑state). | Tool – emergent from ignorance. |
| **Thermodynamics** | Probabilities enter via the partition function \(Z=\sum e^{-\beta E}\); they encode how many micro‑states correspond to a macro‑state. | Tool – counts micro‑states. |
| **Stochastic processes** (Brownian motion, Langevin dynamics) | Random noise models interactions with an unresolved environment. | Tool – effective description. |
| **Quantum field theory (perturbation theory)** | Feynman diagrams involve probabilistic amplitudes; cross‑sections are obtained by squaring and integrating. | Tool – computational convenience, but underlying theory can be formulated deterministically (path integral). |
| **Quantum mechanics (Copenhagen)** | Wavefunction evolves unitarily; measurement outcome probabilities are given by the Born rule; no underlying deterministic state is assumed. | **Fundamental** in this interpretation. |
| **Quantum mechanics (de Broglie–Bohm)** | Deterministic particle trajectories guided by a pilot wave; the Born rule emerges from an ensemble of hidden positions. | Probabilities are *epistemic*. |
| **Quantum mechanics (many‑worlds)** | All outcomes occur in a branching multiverse; probabilities are subjective measures over branches. | Probabilities arise from decision theory, not from a stochastic process. |
| **Quantum mechanics (objective collapse)** | Random collapses happen with a certain rate; the probability distribution is built into the collapse dynamics. | Probabilities are *objective* but still *derived* from a stochastic rule. |
| **Classical gravity (GR)** | No probabilities; deterministic Einstein equations. | Not statistical. |

> **Bottom line:**  
> In every field except for the *interpretation* of standard quantum mechanics, probabilities are a *statistical tool*—they encode ignorance or coarse‑graining over a deeper, deterministic reality.  Only the Copenhagen interpretation makes them *ontic* (fundamental), but this is a matter of philosophical choice, not a physical necessity.

## 2.  What makes QM special?

1. **Superposition principle** – The state vector can be a linear combination of orthogonal states.  The probability of obtaining a particular outcome is *quadratic* in the amplitudes: \(P=|\langle a|\psi\rangle|^{2}\).  No classical statistical mixture of the same states gives the same statistics.  

2. **Measurement postulate** – The unitary evolution of the wavefunction is interrupted by a *non‑unitary* projection that yields an outcome.  The theory does *not* explain *why* the projection happens, only *what* the probabilities are.  

3. **Non‑commutativity** – The outcomes of successive measurements depend on the order; this non‑classical structure cannot be reproduced by a purely statistical description without underlying hidden variables.  

Because of these features, the standard Copenhagen interpretation declares probabilities to be *irreducible*.  In alternative interpretations, one adds extra structure (hidden variables, many worlds, collapse dynamics) that removes the irreducibility and re‑introduces a deterministic layer.

## 3.  DDFH’s view

* DDFH proposes that the hidden field \(\mathcal Q\) on a compact 2‑D manifold follows a deterministic, gradient‑like flow.  
* The *observable* statistics (Born rule, Bell correlations, no‑signalling) arise from the **projection** of this deterministic trajectory onto discrete outcomes.  
* Therefore, DDFH treats probabilities as **emergent**, not fundamental.  
* In this sense, DDFH **does not** add a new theory where probabilities are fundamental; it *explains* why QM’s probabilities look fundamental while actually being statistical consequences of an underlying deterministic dynamics.

## 4.  Summary

| Question | Answer |
|----------|--------|
| Is QM the only theory that treats probabilities as fundamental? | **No.**  The standard Copenhagen interpretation does, but other interpretations and most other physical theories treat probability as an epistemic or emergent tool. |
| Do all statistical calculations implicitly acknowledge statistics is just a tool? | Yes.  In statistical mechanics, thermodynamics, stochastic dynamics, and even in QFT (when you write down scattering cross‑sections), probabilities are used to summarize an ensemble of micro‑states or to model coarse‑grained interactions, not to *define* reality. |
| Where does the “fundamental statistics” idea come from? | It is a **interpretational choice**.  Some interpretations (Copenhagen, objective‑collapse) posit an irreducible randomness, while others (Bohmian, many‑worlds, DDFH) attribute the statistics to deeper, deterministic layers. |

Thus, **DDFH is not unique** in claiming that probabilities are not fundamental; it actually *contradicts* that claim by providing a deterministic substrate that reproduces quantum statistics.  The unique feature of standard QM is that its most popular interpretation takes those probabilities as a basic postulate, but that is a *philosophical stance* rather than an inescapable physical requirement.
