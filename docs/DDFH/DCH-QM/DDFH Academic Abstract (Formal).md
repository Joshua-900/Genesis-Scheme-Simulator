

The Deterministic Dimensional Flow Hypothesis (DDFH) proposes that the probabilistic phenomena observed in quantum mechanics are emergent artifacts of modeling systems through an overfitted three-dimensional spatial framework. Rather than interpreting quantum uncertainty, superposition, and non-locality as intrinsic properties of reality, DDFH posits that these arise from dimensional relational constraints at quantum scales, where degrees of freedom dynamically reduce due to geometric compactification. As systems scale up, these constraints relax, enabling classical locality and determinism to emerge naturally through a process of dimensional unfolding.

Within this framework, Quantum Field Theory (QFT) is reinterpreted as a projection model that captures consistent relational behaviors of a deeper, deterministic geometric substrate. The extraordinary precision of quantum predictions is thus viewed not as validation of ontological randomness, but as evidence of a stable causal structure whose projection artifacts behave consistently within our observational constraints.

DDFH bridges the gap between quantum and classical regimes by introducing scale-dependent dimensional accessibility as the mechanism underlying the quantum-classical transition. Entanglement correlations are recontextualized as expressions of hidden geometric adjacency, and probabilistic modeling is reframed as a causal abstraction compensating for inaccessible relational variables.

The hypothesis aligns with insights from the Holographic Principle, String Theory compactification, and Causal Dynamical Triangulations, proposing a unified geometric foundation beneath quantum phenomena. DDFH offers experimental pathways through confined quantum systems, mesoscopic scaling studies, and theoretical simulations designed to probe projection-induced anomalies, aiming to refine, not replace, the predictive power of modern physics.
